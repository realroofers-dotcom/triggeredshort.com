Opinion · Mark Nejmeh

Everybody is an investor now

Four million American children were signed up for investment accounts before most of them could walk. Nobody is teaching them to read the paperwork. That is what this site is for.

I have spent nearly fifty years in a trade you cannot do from a keyboard. I know exactly what a wage is worth and what it costs to earn one.

What I think is coming is this. As artificial intelligence takes work that used to belong to people, the wage stops being the main way an ordinary person gets ahead. What a person owns starts to matter more than what they earn. I do not say that happily. I say it because I think it is true, and because pretending otherwise helps nobody.

If that is where this goes, then the ability to read what you own stops being a hobby for people who like the markets. It becomes something closer to a survival skill — and it is the one thing almost nobody is taught.

Four million children were signed up before they could walk. In eighteen years they will own something and be expected to understand it. Nobody is teaching them how, and nobody taught their parents either.

That is what this site is for. Not stock tips. Not a newsletter telling you what to buy. A place where a person who owns something — a retirement account, shares from a job, money they cannot afford to lose — can find out what the paperwork actually says, in words they can read, for a price they can pay.

I want to be careful about one thing, because it matters. I am not saying investing is the only way up. If it were, everyone without money to spare would be finished before they start, and I do not believe that and would not build for it. What I am saying is narrower and I think more useful: for the people who are already in this — whether they chose it or not — the gap is information, and that gap can be closed.

— Mark Nejmeh
Roofer since 1978. Founder, JobCreation.us.

The facts underneath it

Everything above is my opinion. What follows is the record, with sources, so you can weigh the one against the other.

On 4 July 2025, Section 70204 of H.R. 1 — Public Law 119-21, commonly called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act — created a new kind of account for American children, named Trump Accounts in the statute. They are a form of traditional individual retirement account, opened on behalf of a child under eighteen who is a U.S. citizen with a Social Security number.

WhatThe rule
The federal depositA one-time $1,000 contribution from the Treasury, for children born 1 January 2025 through 31 December 2028.
Who can open oneAny child under eighteen with a Social Security number. The $1,000 is limited to the birth years above.
How it is claimedA parent or guardian files Form 4547 with the IRS. One page.
Who else can pay inAnyone — parents, grandparents, friends, employers — up to a combined $5,000 a year, indexed for inflation after 2027. The federal $1,000 does not count against that cap.
EmployersMay contribute, and employees may fund an account through pre-tax payroll deduction.
Where the money goesInvested in low-cost index funds and held through what the statute calls the growth period, ending 31 December of the year the child turns seventeen.
Guidance so farIRS Notice 2025-68 in December 2025; proposed regulations issued 6 March 2026. Accounts began accepting contributions 5 July 2026.

Take-up, as reported by the IRS

By 31 March 2026, more than four million children had been signed up, with more than one million covered by elections for the $1,000 contribution. By mid-June 2026 enrolment had passed six million children, with roughly 1.4 million qualifying for the federal deposit — about 39% of eligible children.

It has been tried before, and measured

The Treasury's own rulemaking cites SEED OK, an Oklahoma programme from 2007 which is unusual in this field for having been built as a randomised controlled trial. Oklahoma automatically opened 529 accounts for the newborns of consenting families in the treatment group and deposited $1,000. Researchers followed the outcomes for more than a decade. Four years in, parents in the treatment group reported higher social-emotional development in their children, and mothers in that group were more likely to use mainstream financial products.

Sources: Public Law 119-21 §70204 · IRS, Trump Accounts, irs.gov/trumpaccounts and TrumpAccounts.gov · IRS newsroom, 4 million children signed up · Federal Register, Trump Accounts Contribution Pilot Program, 9 March 2026 · IRS Notice 2025-68. Figures are as reported on the dates given and will have moved since.

This site takes no position on the politics of that law, and it is not the point of this page. Supporters call it a head start. Critics have argued the money is small against the cost of raising a child, that it favours families who can afford to add the further $5,000, and that a retirement account is a strange vehicle for a newborn. Those are real arguments and this is not the place to settle them. What is not in dispute is the number: millions of American children now hold an investment account.

What that means for how this site is built

Every company here gets the same measurements, computed from its own filings: whether it added people or shed them, what investors put in against what is left, what business it says it is in, and how many of its patents are actually alive. Nobody here assigns those labels. A company changes its own by what it files.

Everything is explained in plain English, once, on the page where it appears — because a term you cannot look up is the same as a term nobody told you.

There are no geniuses on Wall Street. Just better informed people.
