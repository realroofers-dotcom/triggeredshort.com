The standard measure

How'm I Doin'?

One question, asked of every company on this site, answered the same way every time.

Inspired by Ed Koch, Mayor of New York City, who asked it on street corners and took whatever answer came back.

A company that sells shares to the public is asking the same question Koch asked, whether it means to or not. It takes money from people and promises to do something worthwhile with it. So the fair thing is to ask, plainly and in public: how'd it go?

The answer needs no expertise to read. It is two numbers.

How much money did investors put in — and what is the whole company worth now?

That is the entire measure. Both numbers are public. Neither can be recalculated into something friendlier. And it works the same on a company worth ten million dollars as on one worth two hundred billion.

The same question, two answers

Money went in. Investors put at least $107 million into this company. The whole thing is now worth about $10 million.
Money came out. IBM has not raised a dollar from shareholders. It has paid dividends without a break since 1916.

Put side by side, the graph stops being about any one company and starts being about a direction. One of these took money from investors. The other gave money to them. Nothing in either chart is an accusation, and nothing in either is an opinion. It is arithmetic, drawn the same way twice.

Why it is not called "wealth destroyed"

Because that phrase starts an argument, and the argument is always the same one: destroyed from which peak, adjusted for which splits, measured against whose shares. Every one of those questions has three defensible answers, which means the number can be talked away.

Money in, against what it is worth now, cannot be talked away. The first figure is what companies announced in their own press releases. The second is the share count times the price. There is nothing to dispute, which is exactly why we use it.

The rules we hold ourselves to

  1. We say "at least." A raised figure is a floor unless we can show it is complete. A floor can only ever be argued upward, and we would rather be corrected in that direction.
  2. Market value is the share count times the price. Both public, neither arguable, no adjustment applied by us.
  3. The receipts are printed on the chart. Every raise, with its date and its amount. Nobody should have to take our word for a total.
  4. No conclusion is drawn for you. The graph does not say a company is good or bad, or that anyone did anything wrong. It says what went in and what is there now.

Every company gets one

This is not a list of targets. Every company with a room on this site gets the same graph, built from the same two numbers, whatever the answer turns out to be. A company that took in money and grew will show that. A company that never raised a dollar will show that. A company that took in a hundred million and has ten million left will show that too.

The point is not the verdict. The point is that the question gets asked of everyone, in the same words, with the answer in public — which is what Koch was doing when he stood on the corner and asked.

There are no geniuses on Wall Street. Just better informed people.
