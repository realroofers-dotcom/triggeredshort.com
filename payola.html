What the rules allow

Wall Street research is governed by FINRA Rule 2241. Three lines matter here, quoted from the rule:

Rule 2241(b)(2)(E). A firm’s policies must “prohibit compensation based upon specific investment banking services transactions.”
Rule 2241(c)(4)(B). A research report must disclose “if the research analyst has received compensation based upon (among other factors) the member’s investment banking revenues.”
Rule 2241(c)(4)(C). A report must disclose if the firm managed an offering for the company in the past 12 months, was paid by it for investment banking in the past 12 months, or “expects to receive or intends to seek” such pay in the next three months.

So an analyst may not be paid for a particular deal — but his pay may rise with the bank’s deal business, as long as the report says so. Those disclosures sit at the back of research reports most investors never see. The fees themselves are public: they are in the prospectus.

One company, from the record: Theriva Biologics

Paid research. When Theriva was called Pipex, it paid Crystal Research Associates $35,000 for an executive information overview while Crystal’s co-founder, Jeffrey Kraws, was a Pipex director and vice president. (Form 10-KSB/A, August 29, 2008)

The lawsuit. Theriva is the defendant in Nejmeh v. Theriva Biologics, Inc., 3:26-cv-00705-ART-CSD (D. Nev.). In its own quarterly report of November 12, 2026, Theriva says the lawsuit made it “aware of the fact that the definitive proxy statement for the 2026 Annual Meeting contained errors,” and that two sections of the proxy “inadvertently identified” its warrant proposal as a routine matter on which brokers could vote. Theriva also says it “intends to vigorously defend itself in this litigation.” On November 18, at a special meeting in Spain, shareholders ratified the warrants: 2,804,211 for, 2,516,522 against, 11,373,054 broker non-votes. (Form 10-Q, Nov 12, 2026; Form 8-K, Nov 18, 2026)
